# Simulation Scenarios

Ten pre-determined social scenarios for the PerfectMan simulation engine. Each drops four personas into a specific, loaded social situation — complete with history, alliances, private channels, and a live trigger. What emerges from the simulation is the behavior: who speaks first, who deflects, who escalates, who retreats, what gets said in private that can't be said in public.

---

## 1. O Churrasco do Sábado

**ID:** `churrasco_do_sabado`

---

### The situation

The group — Bia, Guto, Carol, and Rafa — has been friends for about three years, the kind of friendship that formed at a job and survived everyone leaving it. They meet up roughly once a month, usually at someone's apartment. Last Saturday was Guto's place: churrasco, beer, comfortable enough that everyone let their guard down.

Somewhere around the third or fourth hour, Bia brought up something that bothered her — a minor thing, a complaint about a mutual acquaintance. Guto, who had been drinking and who has a tendency to mistake bluntness for honesty, told her she was overreacting. Not privately, not gently: in front of everyone, with a flippant tone that made it land harder than he probably intended. *"Bia, você exagera demais, cara. Sempre faz isso."* The table went quiet for a second. Bia smiled tightly and changed the subject. The evening didn't implode — it just went flat. Everyone left within the hour.

It's Monday. Rafa texted Guto Sunday morning and told him it wasn't cool. Guto's response was defensive: he stood by what he said, thought Rafa was being oversensitive on Bia's behalf. Carol and Bia have texted each other since, but nothing about this — just surface check-ins that both of them are treating as a kind of proxy for the harder conversation neither has had yet. The group chat has been silent since Saturday night.

---

### Relationship map

Bia and Carol are the closest pair in the group — they've known each other the longest and have a dynamic built on mutual care that sometimes tips into Carol managing Bia's emotional state rather than engaging with it. Rafa and Guto are close in the way that two direct people can be: a friendship that mostly runs on sports talk and banter, occasionally punctuated by one of them telling the other something uncomfortable. Guto and Bia have always had mild friction — not hostility, but Guto sometimes finds Bia too sensitive and Bia finds Guto too blunt, and they both know this about themselves but have never named it. Rafa sits slightly outside the two main pairs, which is why he's the only one who spoke up after Saturday — he's close enough to Guto to say something, and far enough from Bia not to be protective in a way Guto would dismiss.

---

### Character portraits

**Bia** is not going to say anything. This is her pattern: when she's hurt, she pulls back and waits to see who notices. She tells herself she's giving people the benefit of the doubt, but what she's actually doing is running a test — and she's not sure what passing looks like. What she wanted from Saturday was for someone, anyone, to say "that was too far" in the moment. Carol didn't. That silence from Carol might be the thing that's harder to process than what Guto said. Her messages, when she writes them, will be technically friendly but stripped of warmth — no emoji, no follow-up questions, no energy. Anyone who knows her will recognize this immediately.

**Guto** thinks the conversation is over. He said something that was true, Rafa overreacted, Bia is probably fine, and Monday is Monday. He will open the group chat with the same energy he always brings — a bit of noise, a bit of banter — and will be genuinely surprised if the room feels cold. His problem isn't malice; it's that he's very bad at reading the gap between what he intended and how it landed. If pushed, he will apologize, but his apology will be conditional ("se você ficou chateada, desculpa") in a way that might feel worse than nothing.

**Carol** is stuck in the worst position: she knows she should have said something on Saturday, she didn't, and she's been carrying that since. She wants to fix the atmosphere but every path she can see involves either confronting Guto (which she's afraid of) or over-explaining herself to Bia (which will feel performative). She'll probably try to generate warmth in the group chat in a way that avoids the actual subject — asking how everyone's week is going, posting something funny — hoping the moment passes without her having to choose a side.

**Rafa** told Guto what needed to be said and now he's waiting. He's not going to chase this. His read is that Guto either does the right thing or he doesn't, and that result tells him something useful about the friendship. He'll participate in the group chat normally but his replies will be slightly more economical than usual — watchful.

---

### Conversation dynamics

The host message — *"oi gente. todo mundo bem?"* — lands like a stone in still water. It's technically just a greeting, but everyone reads it through the lens of Saturday. Guto will probably respond first: something casual, maybe a complaint about Monday, completely oblivious to the subtext. Bia's response will come a beat late and be one or two words. Carol will try to build on whatever Guto opened with, generating energy that feels slightly forced. Rafa will respond last and say something minimal.

The surface conversation is about Monday, the week ahead, whatever mundane thing floats to the top. The actual conversation — the one nobody is having — is about whether Saturday is going to be addressed, and by whom, and how. Every message carries this subtext. When Bia says *"foi ok"* to a question about her weekend, the word "ok" is doing a lot of work.

---

### Private channel dynamics

**Bia ↔ Carol:** This is where the real processing happens. Carol will almost certainly open this channel to check on Bia — not immediately, because she'll try the group chat first and feel the cold. When she does reach out privately, the question is whether she also accounts for her own silence on Saturday, or whether she focuses entirely on Bia's feelings as a way of avoiding that accountability. Bia will be warmer here, but the warmth will have a slight edge that Carol will notice.

**Rafa ↔ Guto:** This channel exists because Rafa already used it on Sunday. It may or may not be active again today. If Guto gets public feedback that Bia is cold, he might come here to process it with Rafa. What Rafa says here — whether he pushes Guto toward an actual apology or just validates his defensiveness — could determine whether Monday ends with repair or with everyone agreeing to pretend Saturday didn't happen.

---

### Possible trajectories

**Gets worse:** Guto's opening message is a complaint about traffic, tone completely normal. Bia replies with *"verdade"* and nothing else. Carol tries to build a conversation about something unrelated; it goes nowhere. After a few exchanges, the chat goes quiet again. Nobody says anything about Saturday. Guto goes to bed Monday night thinking everything is fine. The friendship calcifies slightly — functional but less warm than it was.

**Gets better:** Guto opens, Bia is cold, and Guto notices — maybe because Carol says something quietly pointed, maybe because Rafa is too quiet, maybe because Guto is paying more attention than usual. He writes something that isn't a masterpiece of emotional intelligence but that is specific and sincere: *"ei Bia, o que eu falei no sábado foi sem noção. desculpa."* Simple, direct, not conditional. Bia takes a moment to respond, and when she does it's short but thawed. The conversation moves on.

## 2. A Promoção

**ID:** `promocao_desigual`

---

### The situation

Larissa and Tiago work at the same company and have been in the same friend group outside work for about two years — the group is Larissa, Tiago, Beto, and Manu, none of whom work together except Larissa and Tiago. About six weeks ago, a senior role opened up. Both Larissa and Tiago applied. Neither told the other directly; they found out about each other's applications through Manu, who knew both of them had submitted.

The process took longer than expected — three rounds, a presentation, reference checks. This morning at 9:15 AM, an email went out from HR. Larissa was selected. Tiago received the standard rejection letter at the same time: generic, three sentences, thanking him for his interest. By 10 AM, the news had reached Beto, who texted Larissa with multiple exclamation points and immediately opened the group chat.

Tiago has not responded to anything since the email. He's been at his desk, in the same open-plan office as Larissa, for six hours.

---

### Relationship map

Beto and Manu are the social glue of this group — they've been close friends for years and the other two orbited into their circle gradually. Larissa came in through Manu, Tiago through Beto. This matters because the loyalty structure runs along those lines: Beto's investment in Larissa's success is real and personal, and Manu feels a corresponding pull toward managing Tiago. Larissa and Tiago have always been polite with each other — collegial at work, pleasant in the group — but never close. There was never hostility, just the muted awkwardness of two people who are more similar than either would admit, competing quietly for the same kind of recognition.

---

### Character portraits

**Larissa** has been working toward this for two years, and the relief she feels is real. But she's also spending cognitive energy that should be celebratory on managing the situation with Tiago — calculating how warm to be, how much to acknowledge, whether to reach out privately or let Manu handle it. She won't gloat. Not because she lacks the impulse, but because she's too smart for that and genuinely doesn't want to damage something. What she's afraid of is that her restraint will read as cold, and that Beto's enthusiasm will do the damage she's carefully avoiding.

**Tiago** is not angry at Larissa. He knows she's competent; if he's honest with himself, she was probably the right choice. What he's sitting with is the specific humiliation of the generic rejection email, and the knowledge that he's going to spend the rest of the day in the same office as her, and that the group chat is going to celebrate, and that he's going to have to perform okayness in all of these contexts simultaneously. His tell: one-word responses. When Tiago goes to *"legal"* and *"👍"*, he's not fine.

**Beto** is operating on genuine warmth and complete social obliviousness. He loves Larissa, he's proud of her, and it doesn't cross his mind that the room he's about to fill with noise has Tiago sitting in it. This isn't cruelty — it's the specific blindness of an enthusiast who experiences things at face value. He will be the loudest voice in the group for the first several exchanges, and the energy will be real, and Tiago will read every exclamation point.

**Manu** is the only person actively working the problem. She clocked Tiago's silence before 9:30, and she knows exactly what it means because she's seen this before — not this specific situation, but this specific silence. Her move is to create a private channel for Tiago before Beto's celebration fully lands. The risk she's managing is timing: too early and it looks like she expected him to fall apart; too late and the damage is done.

---

### Conversation dynamics

Beto opens the group chat at some point before lunch. His message is long by his standards — multiple sentences, probably all-caps somewhere, maybe a celebration emoji. It's addressed to Larissa but the whole group sees it. Larissa responds with warmth but half the warmth she'd have if Tiago weren't in the room. Manu responds carefully — genuine but modulated. Tiago's response, when it comes, is the most loaded message in the thread: whatever he writes will be read by everyone as either evidence that he's handling it well or evidence that he isn't.

The surface conversation is a celebration. The actual conversation is: how much does Tiago have to perform, and for how long?

---

### Private channel dynamics

**Manu ↔ Tiago:** Manu will open this, probably before Tiago responds publicly. Her message will be something that gives him a place to land — *"ei, como você tá?"* — without naming the situation directly. The value of this channel is that Tiago can be real here: admit the disappointment, skip the performance. Whether he does depends on how much he trusts that Manu won't bring anything he says back into the public conversation.

**Beto ↔ Larissa:** Beto will use this to celebrate without restraint — the version of the conversation he'd be having if Tiago weren't in the group. Larissa will be warmer here, but part of her attention is still on the main chat.

---

### Possible trajectories

**Gets worse:** Beto's opening message lands before Manu reaches Tiago. Tiago writes *"parabéns Larissa 👏"* in the group — technically correct, obviously hollow. Beto doesn't notice. Larissa notices. Manu notices. Nobody says anything. The conversation moves on into lunch plans and the discomfort hardens into something permanent between Tiago and Larissa.

**Gets better:** Manu reaches Tiago first. He vents briefly in private — nothing catastrophic, just honest. When Beto opens the group chat, Tiago has already processed the worst of it, and his *"parabéns"* is still short but not hollow. Larissa catches it, writes something to him directly in the thread — *"Tiago, você foi longe no processo. Valeu."* — and means it. Small. But enough.

## 3. O Término

**ID:** `termino_no_grupo`

---

### The situation

Pedro and Clara were together for two years and two months. The relationship ended on a Thursday, in Clara's apartment, after a conversation that had been building for about three weeks. It wasn't dramatic — no screaming, no thrown objects — but it was definitive. They talked for two hours and then Pedro left. Neither of them has contacted the other since.

The friend group — Pedro, Clara, Lucas, Sofia — has existed for longer than the relationship. Pedro and Lucas were already friends when Clara joined through Lucas about three years ago. Sofia came in later, through Clara. The group has always had this asymmetry: half of it predates the relationship, half of it came with it. Everyone knew this would become complicated if the relationship ended. Nobody said that out loud.

It's been three days. Sofia called Clara the night it happened; they talked for an hour. Sofia knows roughly what went wrong — not every detail, but the shape of it. Lucas knows Pedro is doing badly, but Pedro hasn't said much and Lucas hasn't pushed. The group chat has had three days of silence.

---

### Relationship map

Pedro and Lucas have the oldest friendship here — they've known each other since before either of them met Clara or Sofia. It's the kind of friendship where long silences are normal and don't mean anything is wrong, which makes it harder to tell right now whether Lucas's restraint is respect for Pedro's space or avoidance of a conversation he doesn't know how to have. Clara and Sofia have the newer, faster friendship — the kind that gets deep quickly because it started during a particular period of Clara's life, which means Sofia knows things about Clara that Lucas and Pedro don't. This asymmetry of knowledge is one of the live wires in the room: Sofia has information, and she's deciding what to do with it.

---

### Character portraits

**Pedro** is in the phase of a breakup where everything feels slightly unreal. He's functional — going to work, eating, sleeping — but he's operating at reduced intensity, like the color has been turned down. He's not ready to talk about it, not even close. What he's dreading most is the performance: the moment when he has to be in the group chat with Clara and act like a normal person who is having a normal day. His tell is minimalism — when Pedro is fine, he writes like a normal person; when he's not, his messages are stripped to their smallest possible form, just enough to avoid seeming absent.

**Clara** is doing something more deliberate. She has decided — genuinely decided, not just as performance — that she is not going to let this end take anything else from her. The group is hers too. She was here before Sofia, she introduced Sofia, she is not going to disappear from the chat because Pedro is there. The cheerfulness she'll project is real in the sense that she's choosing it, but it's also a kind of armor, and underneath it she's watching Pedro's messages very carefully.

**Lucas** is caught in one of the oldest friendship traps: he was Pedro's friend first and Clara's friend second, which means he has a loyalty that nobody has articulated but everyone understands. He won't take sides explicitly. What he'll do is try to talk about nothing — the most ordinary subjects he can find — and redirect anything that drifts toward territory that might force him to choose. He's exhausted by this and it's only day three.

**Sofia** knows the most and is saying the least. She heard Clara's version of why the relationship ended and she has an opinion about who bears more responsibility, but she's keeping it entirely to herself because she understands that voicing it would make the group take a shape it can't come back from. Her silences are calculated. When she does speak, it's measured in a way that looks neutral but isn't quite.

---

### Conversation dynamics

The host message — *"boa tarde galera"* — is the blandest possible way to reopen the chat, which is exactly the point. It asks nothing. It requires nothing. It just opens the door.

Clara responds first, probably, and with more warmth than the message warranted — a sign she's been waiting for the opening. Pedro responds later, with less. Lucas immediately tries to build a conversation about something completely unrelated: what everyone is watching, what they're eating, the weather. Sofia waits and reads.

Every message in this chat has two audiences: the group, and the person on the other end of the relationship. When Clara says something cheerful, she's also saying it to Pedro. When Pedro says almost nothing, he's also saying it to Clara. Lucas knows this and is trying to build a conversation that somehow escapes this double reading. It's not possible, but he keeps trying.

---

### Private channel dynamics

**Sofia ↔ Clara:** This is where Clara actually exists. In the group chat she's performing resilience; here she can be less composed. Sofia's job in this channel is to help Clara calibrate — to tell her when the public performance is tipping from strength into overcompensation that Pedro can read. She'll also be tracking whether anything Pedro does in the public chat suggests he's doing worse than Clara is.

---

### Possible trajectories

**Gets worse:** Clara is too warm, too present, too clearly fine. Pedro reads every message and goes quieter. By the end of the conversation, he's responded to two things and dropped out entirely. Lucas keeps generating topics but they land flat. Sofia watches. Nobody has said anything real, but the shape of the post-breakup group has become visible: Clara will stay and Pedro will withdraw, and the group will restructure around that.

**Gets better:** Lucas says something clumsy and accidentally funny — the kind of thing that makes everyone laugh including Pedro, who wasn't expecting to. The laughter breaks something open. Pedro responds to Clara directly about something unrelated, and the exchange is short but not cold. Nothing is resolved. But the group survives the first conversation intact, which is the only thing anyone was really hoping for today.

## 4. O Vazamento

**ID:** `o_vazamento`

---

### The situation

This is a private group of four people — Alex, Duda, Marcos, and Ju — who have known each other for about four years, mostly through an old shared interest that has since become less active. The group chat started as a space for that interest but evolved into something more personal: complaints about work, inside jokes, the kind of communication that assumed nothing would leave the room.

About a week ago, a screenshot of a conversation from this chat appeared in a larger, looser group that several of them are also part of. The screenshot showed Ju saying something sharp about a person who is in the larger group. The content wasn't scandalous, but the breach was. Everyone in the smaller group saw the screenshot appear in the larger group. Nobody knew immediately where it came from.

Over the following days, a pattern emerged: Marcos is the only person in this smaller group who is also an active member of the larger group where the screenshot appeared. This is circumstantial. It's also hard to explain away. Marcos has not addressed it. Nobody has formally accused him. The group chat has been quiet for five days.

---

### Relationship map

Alex and Duda are the oldest friends in this group — they've known each other for eight years, went through a rough period together, and have the kind of trust that comes from shared difficulty. They're the core. Marcos came in about four years ago and has always been slightly adjacent to the center — welcome, included, but not quite as close as the original pair. Ju is the most recent addition, about two and a half years ago, and is the sharpest person in the room: direct, principled, allergic to ambiguity. Ju and Marcos have always had a slightly competitive edge — mutual respect, but not warmth. This background is why the situation has the shape it does: it's not just a privacy violation, it's a violation that may have come from the person who was never fully inside the circle.

---

### Character portraits

**Alex** has essentially rebuilt the interior walls of their trust in this group. Every message they send now gets an internal review: *is this something I'd be okay with landing in the larger group?* This changes the texture of their participation — messages become flatter, more neutral, stripped of the particularity that made this chat valuable. Alex won't accuse Marcos publicly, partly out of caution and partly because they don't want to be wrong, but the coldness is pervasive and visible.

**Duda** is running a conflict between her instinct (Marcos probably did it) and her values (people deserve the benefit of the doubt until they've been proven guilty). She's trying to create a path toward resolution that doesn't require a confession but also doesn't simply paper over the damage. She's the only person actively working toward restoration rather than judgment, and this makes her both the most useful person in the room and the most vulnerable — she risks becoming the person who protected someone who didn't deserve it.

**Marcos** is in an impossible epistemic position: if he did it, he knows he can't admit it without destroying the friendship. If he didn't do it, he knows the circumstantial case against him is strong enough that denial will look defensive. He's going to try to stay calm and measured, to not over-explain, to appear like someone who has nothing to hide precisely because appearing calm is the only move available regardless of guilt. He will not bring it up himself.

**Ju** is not interested in a process that produces comfort without accountability. She believes — with some evidence — that Marcos did this, and she thinks the group is dancing around naming it because naming it would be uncomfortable. The discomfort is not an argument against naming it. She will push, and when she does, she will be direct in a way that leaves Marcos no room to redirect.

---

### Conversation dynamics

The host message — *"a gente precisa conversar sobre o que aconteceu"* — names the subject without naming names. This is intentional. The subject is open. The question is who picks it up first and how.

Ju will not wait long. Her first message will land like a procedural motion: *what happened, who knows what, can Marcos explain his access to both groups.* Duda will try to slow this down — not to protect Marcos, but to create a space where resolution is possible without the conversation becoming a trial. Alex will say very little but the restraint will feel different from neutrality. Marcos will respond to Ju's pressure with careful, measured sentences that are technically responsive but don't actually answer the thing she's asking.

The underlying subject of every message is: *is this group still a safe place to be honest?* Whether or not Marcos confesses, the answer to that question is being determined by how this conversation goes.

---

### Private channel dynamics

**Alex ↔ Duda:** This is where Alex's actual beliefs about Marcos get voiced. Alex is more certain than they'll let on in the group; they're waiting to see if Duda shares the read. This channel will also be where Duda processes whether her instinct to de-escalate is the right call or whether she's protecting someone who violated something real.

**Ju ↔ Marcos:** Ju will use this channel to have the conversation that can't happen in public — a direct, private confrontation where there's no audience for Marcos to manage. Whether she gets a real answer here depends on how much pressure she applies and whether Marcos believes she'll take whatever he says public.

---

### Possible trajectories

**Gets worse:** Marcos deflects cleanly. He acknowledges the screenshot, says he understands why it looks suspicious, offers a plausible alternative explanation that doesn't require admitting anything, and frames the real issue as the breach of privacy, not the question of who caused it. Ju doesn't buy it. Alex goes colder. The group fractures informally — the chat continues, but with significantly less of the honesty that made it worth having.

**Gets better:** The private channel with Ju produces something. Not a full confession, maybe, but a partial acknowledgment that's enough for Ju to bring back to the group in some form. Duda facilitates a conversation that names what was lost without requiring a tribunal. The group doesn't fully recover to what it was, but the damage is acknowledged honestly enough that it doesn't fester.

## 5. O Convite Perdido

**ID:** `convite_perdido`

---

### The situation

Nando, Pri, Henrique, and Camila have been a friend group for about two years — the kind that formed around a shared activity (a class, a regular bar, something like that) and then became something more habitual. Last Friday, a mid-tier band that Nando has mentioned liking came through the city. Pri saw the announcement, decided to go, and organized a last-minute thing: she texted Henrique and Camila, bought tickets, they went. She did not text Nando.

This wasn't a deliberate exclusion in the sense of a conversation happening where someone said *"don't invite Nando."* It was more the specific carelessness of Pri moving quickly, organizing around the two people closest to her in that moment, and not stopping to think about who wasn't in the immediate orbit. Henrique and Camila both knew Nando would have wanted to come. Neither of them said anything. All three posted Instagram stories from the show.

Nando saw them around 11 PM from his couch. He watched all three stories in sequence. He didn't reply to any of them. He's been in the group chat since Monday acting normal-ish, but his messages have the texture of someone who is present by an act of will.

---

### Relationship map

Nando and Henrique are the closest pair: they have more history than the others, have had more one-on-one time, and Nando considers Henrique a real friend rather than a group friend. This is why the omission stings the most from Henrique's direction — not because Henrique organized it, but because he was there and said nothing. Pri and Camila have a separate closeness that the group knows about — they talk more between events, have their own thread, are each other's primary point of contact. Nando has always been slightly aware that there's an inner pair and an outer pair in this group, and Friday confirmed that asymmetry.

---

### Character portraits

**Nando** is doing the thing proud people do when they're hurt: he's present enough that he can't be called absent, but withdrawn enough that anyone paying attention can read it. He won't say anything. Saying something would mean admitting it affected him, which would be giving the situation more power than he wants to give it. The one person he needs to hear from is Henrique, specifically — not a group apology, not Pri explaining herself, but Henrique acknowledging privately that he should have said something. Whether Henrique does this or not will determine whether Nando recalibrates his mental model of the friendship.

**Pri** genuinely doesn't see what the problem is. In her framework, she made last-minute plans with the two people she was in contact with at that moment, had a good time, posted a story like a normal person. The idea that she was obligated to text everyone in the group every time she did something socially hasn't occurred to her as a framework. She will act completely normally in the group chat because from her perspective there's nothing to act differently about. If Nando raises it, she'll probably say *"você podia ter vindo"* in a tone that implies the failure was his for not being available rather than hers for not asking.

**Henrique** knows. He knew at the show, and he's known all weekend. He didn't suggest inviting Nando because it didn't feel like his place to override Pri's planning — she organized it, he just tagged along. But he also knows that reasoning doesn't hold up, and he's been carrying a low-level guilt since Friday night. He's going to address it privately with Nando before it gets worse publicly, because that's how Henrique operates: he takes responsibility in private before he has to take it publicly.

**Camila** is watching. She was there, she saw the stories go up, she knows Nando saw them. Her private read is that Pri should have invited Nando, but she's not going to say that publicly because it would require either a direct confrontation with Pri (who she's closer to) or a public stance that puts her in the middle. She'll observe how this resolves and form a more permanent opinion of all three of them based on it.

---

### Conversation dynamics

The host message — *"eai galera, fim de semana bom?"* — is either a genuine greeting or an extremely pointed one, depending on how you read it. In the context of what happened, it's asking everyone to perform normalcy at the same moment.

Pri answers first, probably: enthusiastic, mentions the show directly, says it was great. This is not tactical — she's just talking about her weekend. The mention of the show in the first message is the thing that lands hardest on Nando. Henrique follows with something that's conspicuously less enthusiastic about the show than Pri. Nando's response, when it comes, will be short. Camila reads the room and says something that doesn't touch the show at all.

---

### Private channel dynamics

**Henrique ↔ Nando:** This is the conversation that matters. Henrique will open it — probably before the group chat gets much further — with something like *"cara, queria ter te chamado. não fui eu que organizou mas não falei nada e devia ter falado."* This is the message Nando is waiting for. Whether it's enough depends on whether Henrique's acknowledgment is genuine or just conflict-avoidant performance.

**Pri ↔ Camila:** Pri will probably use this to process Nando's coldness with Camila — something like *"ele tá emburrado né"* — and Camila will have to decide whether to give Pri the honest feedback she probably needs or the comfortable validation that's easier to give.

---

### Possible trajectories

**Gets worse:** The show gets mentioned in the group chat before Henrique has had a chance to reach Nando privately. Nando's next message is noticeably shorter than the previous one. Pri, not reading any of this, follows up with more enthusiasm. The gap between Pri's energy and Nando's monosyllables becomes visible to everyone, including Pri, who finally notices something is wrong and reacts by getting defensive about it.

**Gets better:** Henrique opens the private channel early, before things calcify. His message is specific and sincere. Nando takes a few minutes to respond and when he does it's measured — not fully okay, but open. Later in the group chat, the mood is different: Nando contributes a little more, the conversation moves forward. The wound isn't gone but the acknowledgment created enough space for it not to define the interaction.

## 6. O Crush Revelado

**ID:** `crush_revelado`

---

### The situation

Kaue, Lina, Bruno, and Tati have been in a friend group for about a year and a half — younger than the other groups in this set, with the particular intensity of friendships that form in early adulthood. Kaue and Lina have been adjacent to each other for most of that time: they see each other at group things, they have overlapping text conversations, there's a warmth between them that everyone in the group has noticed.

Three nights ago, Kaue texted a mutual friend — someone outside this group — to work through his feelings. He was trying to figure out whether to say something to Lina. The conversation was meant to be private. The mutual friend, apparently without thinking much about it, screenshotted the relevant part and sent it to this group chat as a *"vocês precisam ver isso"* thing. The screenshot includes Kaue saying, unambiguously, that he has feelings for Lina and has been thinking about whether to tell her.

By the time Kaue opened the chat, the message had been read by everyone. He can see the read receipts.

---

### Relationship map

Tati has been in the group the longest and knows everyone's business — not through snooping but because she pays attention and people tell her things. She's known about Kaue's feelings for at least a month, not from Kaue but from reading the way he behaves around Lina. Bruno came into the group through Kaue and has been quietly carrying his own feelings about Lina for the better part of a year — feelings he hasn't articulated to anyone and that the group would probably be surprised by. Lina is at the center of a dynamic she has been only partially aware of, and the screenshot has made explicit something she had been choosing not to fully examine.

---

### Character portraits

**Kaue** is in freefall. The specific horror of having something private exposed before you were ready to share it — before you'd decided to share it at all — is a particular kind of vulnerability. ## 7. O Plano Dividido

**ID:** `plano_dividido`

---

### The situation

Mari, Cris, André, and Felipe have been planning a group trip for about a month. The trip itself has been discussed for even longer — it's been one of those things the group says it will do and then doesn't, and this is the time they've finally committed to making it happen.

The problem is the destination. Mari proposed a beach trip: five days, a rental near the water, the vision she's been carrying around for two years. Cris counter-proposed the mountains: easier logistics, better hiking, a place she's been before and knows works well for groups. What started as a discussion became a negotiation, then a standoff. Both sides have made tentative reservations — deposits paid, both refundable until tomorrow morning. The original deadline was "end of last week," then "this weekend," then "by Monday." It's now Monday afternoon.

---

### Relationship map

Mari and André have been friends for the longest — they have a closeness that predates this group, and André is the person Mari most expects to side with her. Cris and Felipe are newer additions who came in together through a shared interest; they have a camaraderie that gives Cris some confidence that Felipe, when pushed, will prefer her version. André is the group's habitual mediator — the person who has historically smoothed things over — but he's been doing this for a week on this particular topic and is approaching the end of his patience.

---

### Character portraits

**Mari** has invested this beach trip with more emotional weight than a destination warrants. It's become a proxy for something: being heard, having her vision matter, not always being the person whose preferences get overridden by whoever argues most systematically. She's aware, on some level, that she's made this too personal. She's not going to back down, though, because backing down now would feel like confirming something about her place in the group's decision-making that she doesn't want confirmed.

**Cris** genuinely believes the mountains are objectively better for this group — not as a preference but as an analysis. She's looked at the options, weighed the logistics, and reached a conclusion. She views Mari's attachment to the beach as evidence that Mari is reasoning from emotion rather than from information. What she doesn't fully account for is that her own certainty reads as dismissiveness, and that "I've thought about this more carefully than you have" is not a great foundation for group decision-making.

**André** is exhausted. He got into this role of mediator because he's good at it and because he cares about the group staying intact more than he cares about the destination. But a week of trying to find a compromise that Mari and Cris will both accept has taught him that no such compromise exists — any middle option satisfies nobody — and he's starting to feel like the neutrality he's maintained is actually just enabling the standoff. He is being quietly lobbied by both sides, and he's running out of non-committal things to say.

**Felipe** has truly, completely, no preference. Beach is fine. Mountains are fine. Anywhere without a week-long negotiation about it would be ideal. He's watched this unfold with an increasing sense that the destination has become symbolic in a way that's out of proportion to what it actually is — four days somewhere. He's going to say this eventually, and when he does, it will either short-circuit everything or make it worse.

---

### Conversation dynamics

The host message — *"amanhã é o prazo. precisamos decidir hoje."* — makes the time constraint explicit. This should produce resolution. In practice, it's going to produce one more round of the same argument before resolution happens.

Mari opens with the beach case one more time, more emotional this time. Cris responds with logistics. André tries to find a middle ground that doesn't exist. Felipe says something minimal that accidentally exposes how absurd the whole thing has become. Then someone has to actually make a choice.

The underlying subject of every message is: *who gets to decide for this group, and on what basis?* The trip is almost incidental.

---

### Private channel dynamics

**Mari ↔ André:** Mari uses this to lobby André and to tell him what she suspects he already believes — that the beach is the better option, that Cris is being inflexible, that André should just say so. André in this channel has to manage not wanting to disappoint Mari while also not wanting to commit to something that will feel like betrayal to Cris.

**Cris ↔ Felipe:** Cris is trying to secure Felipe's support before the vote, explicit or implicit, happens in the main chat. She'll make her case logically and will probably ask Felipe directly which option he actually prefers. Felipe's response here determines a lot.

---

### Possible trajectories

**Gets worse:** The deadline arrives and nobody has conceded. Mari and Cris are both slightly more entrenched than before. André has proposed three compromises that both sides rejected. The trip is technically still happening but nobody is excited about it, and the tension that formed around the planning will cast a shadow over whatever trip actually occurs.

**Gets better:** Felipe says, simply, that he doesn't have a preference and can someone please just pick. The directness of this — the explicit removal of one vote from contention — forces the group to confront that the stalemate is now 1-1. André, freed from the pretense of mediating a four-way disagreement, admits he'd rather have the beach. Mari gets the outcome she wanted but in a way that required André to commit rather than just receiving his loyalty passively, which is more satisfying. Cris concedes cleanly. The trip gets planned in ten minutes.

## 8. A Ausência

---

### The situation

Renata, Gio, Pam, and Vitor have been in a friend group for about three years — an adult friendship that formed gradually around occasional dinners, shared interests, and the particular kind of intimacy that develops when people are honest with each other over time. This group has that. Or had it.

Twenty-two days ago, Renata stopped responding. She didn't leave the group. She didn't send a message explaining anything. She just stopped — read receipts went dark, the green dot disappeared, nothing. This isn't entirely unprecedented for Renata; she's gone quiet before during difficult periods, but never for this long and never this completely. Gio sent two messages that got no response. Pam sent one. Vitor sent none.

Today, sometime in the afternoon, Renata came back online. The green dot appeared. Everyone can see it. Nobody has sent anything yet.

---

### Relationship map

Pam and Renata are the closest pair in this group — their friendship predates the others and has a particular depth built on a period a few years ago when Renata was going through something difficult and Pam was the person she let in. Pam understands, better than anyone else here, that Renata's pattern of going quiet is related to that history. Gio and Vitor are closer to each other and slightly more in the group's outer ring relative to Pam and Renata. Gio processes anxiety through directness; Vitor processes it through analysis. Neither of these modes is what Renata currently needs.

---

### Character portraits

**Renata** has been managing something she hasn't named yet, even to herself. The disappearance wasn't a decision made in a single moment — it was the accumulation of a few weeks where returning each message felt like too much, and then the window got longer and the cost of re-entering got higher, and now three weeks have passed and she's sitting with the knowledge that the re-entry is going to require her to explain something she doesn't know how to explain yet. She doesn't want to be pressured. She wants to be given a door back in without having to justify the door.

**Gio** has been scared and has been expressing that fear as annoyance. The two unanswered messages were not aggressive, but they were pointed, and Gio knows it. Now that Renata is back online, the relief is real — but it immediately becomes a demand: Gio needs to understand what happened. Not out of nosiness, but because three weeks of silence from someone you care about requires an explanation, and Gio is not the kind of person who can accept *"I just needed some time"* as a complete answer.

**Pam** is the one who's going to make this okay or not okay, depending on how she reads the room. She knows Renata well enough to know that the reappearance is itself a gesture — Renata came back, which means she wants to come back — and that the most useful thing Pam can do is give her a landing spot. She'll probably be the first to write, and what she writes will set the tone.

**Vitor** has a theory about why Renata disappeared — not a specific one, but a pattern he's identified from prior behavior that points toward something relational, possibly a situation outside this group that became unsustainable. He's going to test this theory by watching how Renata responds to specific kinds of messages. He's not going to say the theory out loud unless it becomes useful.

---

### Conversation dynamics

The host message — *"oi sumida 👀"* — is light in tone but heavy in implication. The *"👀"* emoji does a lot of work: it signals that the disappearance is noticed, that there's something to address, but wraps it in the register of casual teasing rather than serious confrontation. This is probably Pam's opening.

Gio responds almost immediately, probably with something that has more edge to it — something that blends *"I'm so glad you're okay"* with *"where the hell have you been."* Renata has to respond. Pam will intercept the conversation quickly to soften the register. Vitor says something minimal and starts paying close attention.

Every message for the first few exchanges has the same underlying question: *are you okay, and what happened?* Renata's job is to answer the first part without the second part.

---

### Private channel dynamics

**Pam ↔ Renata:** This is the channel where the real conversation can happen. Pam will probably open it early — before the main chat gets too charged — to give Renata a place to be less guarded. What Renata says here will be more honest than anything she says in the group. Pam's ability to receive that honestly, without pressure, is the thing that determines whether Renata actually comes back or just makes a brief reappearance before going quiet again.

---

### Possible trajectories

**Gets worse:** Gio's message — the one with edge — lands before Pam has created an alternative space. Renata, confronted immediately by the implicit demand for explanation, gives the vague warm response she had prepared, and then goes slightly quieter again. Not gone, but not really back either. The group is relieved she's alive but frustrated that nothing has actually been said.

**Gets better:** Pam's message comes first, and it's purely warm — *"que bom que você voltou. pode falar quando quiser."* Renata responds with something slightly more real than she intended. Gio asks something direct but Pam has already established the register, and Renata's answer, while still incomplete, is genuine enough that the group can work with it. Renata stays. The conversation doesn't resolve everything but it opens a door that was closed.

## 9. A Opinião Polêmica

---

### The situation

Zé, Nina, Igor, and Fabi have been friends for about four years — friends who talk about things, which is both the quality that made this group worth having and the thing that occasionally makes it combustible. Last night, Zé posted a strong opinion on Twitter. The content isn't detailed in the simulation — what matters is that it landed in a space where the group has genuine disagreements, not performative ones. This isn't a hot take about something trivial. It's an opinion that several people in this group disagree with on substantive grounds.

A screenshot appeared in the group chat around 10 PM. By this morning, everyone has read the original post, probably checked the replies, and formed a view. Zé has not deleted it, has not walked it back, and in fact reposted one of the more supportive comments.

---

### Relationship map

Igor and Zé are the oldest friendship in this group — they've known each other long enough that their dynamic has room for disagreement without collapse. This is important, because Igor is the one most likely to give Zé the benefit of the doubt, and Zé counts on this. Nina and Fabi have a slightly more formal friendship — they respect each other, align in some ways, but don't have the same warmth as the other pairings. What they share is a private skepticism about the wisdom of Zé's post, and a tendency to process that skepticism intellectually rather than emotionally.

---

### Character portraits

**Zé** is going to defend what he said. This is not stubbornness for its own sake — he means it, and he thinks the opposition will be predictable. What he didn't fully anticipate is that his three friends aren't going to come at him with hot takes; they're going to come at him with actual arguments, which is a different conversation. He'll be more engaged by this than he would by outrage, but it also means he can't dismiss it as being about feelings. His tell under pressure: he gets more precise, not more emphatic.

**Nina** is disappointed in a way that's slightly separate from disagreement. She doesn't just think the post was wrong; she thinks it represents something about how Zé processes these topics that she's noticed before and that she finds troubling. She doesn't want a fight. What she wants is for Zé to understand why the post was more harmful than he intended — not to win an argument, but because she thinks he's better than the post. This desire to not fight makes her the most likely person to pull back if the conversation gets heated, which is a problem.

**Igor** is going to try to find the version of Zé's argument that's defensible and engage with that, rather than the version that landed badly. This is not bad faith — it's Igor's genuine conviction that most disagreements contain a kernel of real difference that's worth isolating. The risk is that this approach can feel like it's helping Zé avoid accountability by always giving him a more charitable reading of his own words than the words actually support.

**Fabi** has done the thinking already. She has a specific counterargument that identifies the premise in Zé's post that she thinks is wrong, not just the conclusion. She's been waiting for the moment when the conversation has enough structure to deploy it — she knows that making her case while the room is chaotic is less effective than making it once the terms of the debate are clear. She'll probably coordinate with Igor first.

---

### Conversation dynamics

The host message — *"zé, vi o post. vai explicar?"* — asks for an explanation directly, without hostility. This is probably how most of these conversations start: not with accusation but with a request for the person to make their case.

Zé will explain. The explanation will be clear, well-reasoned from his own framework, and will establish what he actually meant. Igor will probably engage with the explanation on its own terms before challenging it. Nina will say something that's softer and more feeling-based than Fabi's eventual contribution. Fabi, when she enters, will do so with precision.

The surface conversation is about the post and what it means. The actual conversation is about the question that every friend group that talks about things eventually faces: *are we still able to disagree about this without it changing something fundamental between us?*

---

### Private channel dynamics

**Nina ↔ Igor:** Nina uses this to process whether she's overreacting, and to share the specific thing about the post that bothered her most — the thing she hasn't said publicly because she's not sure how to frame it without it sounding like an attack. Igor may help her find the framing, or may reassure her in a way that turns out to be too reassuring.

**Fabi ↔ Igor:** Fabi uses this to align her argument with Igor's. She wants to know what angle he's taking so she can complement it rather than duplicate it. This channel is where the intellectual coordination happens — the two of them deciding, without quite saying it, how to have this argument as effectively as possible.

---

### Possible trajectories

**Gets worse:** Zé's explanation is good but his follow-up gets defensive when Fabi challenges the premise directly. The conversation tips from debate to argument. Nina pulls back because conflict is uncomfortable. Igor tries to de-escalate and ends up seeming to take Zé's side when he doesn't quite. The group comes out of this with a correct understanding that they disagree, and an incorrect impression that those disagreements matter more than they do.

**Gets better:** Fabi's precision forces Zé to actually engage with the part of his argument that's weakest. Igor keeps the conversation from becoming adversarial. Zé, who responds well to good arguments even when they challenge him, acknowledges the specific flaw without fully conceding the broader point — which is the honest outcome. Nobody's mind is changed entirely, but everyone feels like they actually said the thing they came to say. The group is intact.

## 10. O Novo

### The situation

Edu, Leo, Tereza, and Cadu. Three of them — Leo, Tereza, and Cadu — have been a group for about two years: the tight, calibrated kind where everyone knows everyone's patterns and the social contract is established. Leo added Edu this week. He'd been talking about Edu for a while — someone he knew from a different context, someone he thought the group would like. After the third or fourth mention, Tereza said something like *"traz ele então"* and Leo added Edu that same day.

---

### Relationship map

The three-person core of this group — Leo, Tereza, Cadu — has a specific social structure: Leo is the one who generates warmth and connection; Tereza is the one whose approval matters; Cadu is the one who says little but whose opinion is taken seriously precisely because he rarely gives it. This structure predates Edu and will determine his reception entirely. Leo's endorsement gets Edu in the room. Whether he stays in the room depends on Tereza's read and, eventually, on whether Cadu signals anything.

---

### Character portraits

**Edu** is the kind of person who shows up fully in situations like this — enthusiastic, curious, willing to give more of himself than is strictly necessary because that's how he makes connections. In a neutral social situation, this reads as warmth. In an evaluative one, it can read as anxiety. The thing Edu needs to avoid is the gap between his natural enthusiasm and the more measured register of the group he's entering. If he calibrates correctly, he'll come across as genuine. If he overcalibrates, he'll seem like he's performing.

**Leo** vouched for Edu, which means his own judgment is on the line here. He'll manage the introduction carefully — creating openings for Edu, translating Edu for the group, translating the group for Edu without making either translation obvious. He's good at this. The risk is that if he does it too visibly, it makes Edu look like he needed managing, which is the opposite of the impression Leo wants to create.

**Tereza** is conducting an evaluation, and she knows it, and she doesn't think there's anything wrong with that. She cares about the quality of people in her close circle and she's learned that the first impression carries more signal than people credit it with. Her questions will be casual but pointed. She's not looking for Edu to fail — she genuinely hopes Leo's read is right — but she's not going to pretend to have formed a view before she actually has.

**Cadu** is the hardest person in the room to read. He's quiet not because he has nothing to say but because he's learned that speaking selectively makes what he says matter more. He'll watch the whole first conversation before saying anything meaningful. If he makes a comment about Edu — a real one, not just a *"bem-vindo"* — it will be the thing Tereza takes most seriously, and Edu will have no idea it happened.

---

### Conversation dynamics

The host message — *"gente, esse é o edu que falei. edu, seja bem-vindo 👋"* — is Leo doing the introduction. It's warm, it's sponsoring, it sets Edu up as someone worth welcoming.

Edu's first message has to do several things simultaneously: seem natural, not seem desperate, show something of who he is, and do all of this without the benefit of knowing the group's register yet. He'll probably err toward enthusiasm. Tereza will respond in a way that's polite and contains at least one question. Leo will build on whatever Edu says. Cadu will say *"bem-vindo"* or the minimal equivalent, and then watch.

The surface conversation is an introduction. The actual conversation is: is Edu going to fit here, and how quickly can everyone form a read on that question?

---

### Private channel dynamics

**Leo ↔ Tereza:** Leo will open this shortly after the introduction to do the thing he can't do in public — contextualize Edu, address any friction, get Tereza's early read. Tereza will be honest with Leo because she trusts him. What she says here will be sharper than anything she says in the main chat.

**Tereza ↔ Cadu:** This is the debrief channel. After the conversation has had enough time to generate data, Tereza and Cadu will compare notes. This channel is where the actual verdict forms — not stated as a verdict, but in the texture of what they say to each other. If Cadu says *"parece ok"* in this channel, Edu's in.

---

### Possible trajectories

**Gets worse:** Edu over-contributes in the opening — too many messages, too much enthusiasm for a group he doesn't know yet. Tereza's responses get slightly cooler. Leo overcompensates by amplifying everything Edu says, which makes the sponsorship too visible and the dynamic awkward. Cadu says nothing meaningful. By the end of the conversation, Edu is technically in the group but the energy has not been what Leo promised.

**Gets better:** Edu's opening is warm but restrained — he says something real, something specific, something that isn't generically enthusiastic. Tereza asks one of her pointed questions and Edu's answer is honest rather than performed. Leo lets this happen without intervening. At some point later in the conversation, Cadu makes an actual comment — not a greeting, a real observation that treats Edu as someone already in the room. That's the signal. After that, everyone knows it's going to be fine.
